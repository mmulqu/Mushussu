CHAPTER 13: SPARAGMOS / DISSOLVING


SCENE 1: FLIGHT INTO THE WOODS

They ran.

Persephone pulled Mike forward through underbrush that tore at exposed skin. He stumbled, she caught him, kept him moving. Behind them: voices. Flashlights cutting through pine branches like searchlights over a prison yard. How many? She didn't turn to count.

The woods were wrong. Too dark, too dense, trees leaning inward like spectators at an execution. Her implant flickered uselessly—no network, no data, just static hissing at the edges of her vision. She blinked it away.

Mike's breathing was wrong too. Shallow, ragged, dissociative. He wasn't here. Not really. His body moved but his mind had evacuated, fled somewhere she couldn't follow. Psychotic break. That's what they called it. Clean clinical language for a consciousness shattered beyond repair.

He was silent. No gasping, no complaint. Just forward motion. His hands were still bloody—James's blood, tacky and brown in the moonlight. He'd wiped them on his jeans but it didn't matter. The smell followed them. Copper and iron and the slaughterhouse truth beneath all human pretense.

A branch snapped behind them. Close.

Persephone stopped, turned, saw: lights through trees, bobbing, coordinated. Not random searchers. A pattern. Organized. They were being herded.

Her implant sparked: *PATTERN RECOGNITION: Convergence vector detected. PROBABILITY OF ESCAPE: 0.03*

She pulled Mike forward. "Keep moving."

He glanced back at her. His face was a ruin—grief, shock, horror braided so tight she couldn't tell them apart. He'd killed a man. Not in war, not in self-defense, but in rage and desperation and compounded grief that had nowhere else to go. And now he was running, fleeing through woods he didn't know, chased by people he couldn't name, toward a future that ended in prison or worse.

"I can't—" Mike whispered.

"Yes you can."

"Iphy—"

"Don't."

But the word was out, hanging between them like smoke. Iphy. Their daughter. Drowned because they were both distracted, both guilty, both destroyed by it. The wound that never closed. The accusation that never stopped.

They reached a small clearing. Moonlight through branches created broken shadows, fragmentary illumination. Persephone heard: voices ahead AND behind.

Surrounded.

Her implant confirmed: *TARGETS: 12-15 individuals. DISPERSAL PATTERN: Encirclement. TIME TO CONVERGENCE: 4-6 minutes*

She looked at Mike, then back the way they came.

"Keep going," she said.


SCENE 2: THE DECISION TO SACRIFICE

Mike stopped. "What?"

"Keep going. I'll draw them away."

"No."

"They want me. Not you." She said it simply, factually. Like stating weather conditions. "They've been tracking ME. The implant. The network. Whatever the fuck this is. James was one of them. They know I'm here."

Mike stared at nothing. His eyes were burnt-out windows. No one home.

"We can't—" His voice cracked. "Can't leave you—"

"You have to survive. That's all that matters now."

"Persephone—"

She looked at him directly. Really looked. Saw: a good man who'd done a terrible thing. Who'd murdered someone to protect her. Who was about to lose everything—freedom, future, sanity—unless she bought him time.

"You need to get to a hospital. Get help. You can't survive this alone." She gestured at him, swaying slightly, dissociated, somewhere else entirely. "I'll draw them away. You run."

His face collapsed. "This isn't—"

"I brought you here. I brought James into your life. This is my responsibility."

"That's not how responsibility works—"

"It is tonight."

Mike surfaced suddenly, eyes focusing. He looked at her. Really looked. First time in years. And in that look was everything they'd never said: the blame, the guilt, the love that curdled into resentment, the daughter they'd both failed, the marriage that died in a backyard pool while neither of them was paying attention.

"Iphy," he whispered.

"I know." Her voice broke. "I know."

He reached for her hand. She let him take it. His palm was cold. Trembling.

"I should have—" he started.

"We both should have."

Voices closer now. Thirty seconds. Maybe less.

She squeezed his hand once. Released it. "Go. NOW."

"Persephone—"

She pushed him. Hard. Toward the trees, toward escape.

He stumbled backward, then turned and ran. Didn't look back. Just fled through underbrush, puppet with cut strings, back to that hollow dissociation where nothing could touch him.

Persephone watched him disappear into darkness.

Then she turned and ran perpendicular—deeper into woods, away from their path, making noise deliberately. Breaking branches. Shouting. Drawing attention like prey animal faking injury to lead predators from the nest.

Behind her, she heard: voices changing direction. Following HER.

It worked.


SCENE 3: THE HUNT

She ran.

Branches tore at her face, her arms, ripping fabric and skin with democracy of violence. Roots tried to catch her ankles. The ground was uneven, treacherous, designed to break things that fled. Her lungs burned. Her implant screamed static.

But god, she was ALIVE.

Most alive when being chased. That's what the prologue bird knew. The warbler fleeing the hawk. The mouse running from the owl. Predator and prey, locked in dance older than thought, older than language, older than the species-narcissism that said humans were different, special, exempt.

She wasn't exempt.

Her implant sparked with corrupted data: *PURSUIT PATTERN: 8-12 individuals. COORDINATION LEVEL: High. PROBABILITY OF—*

She blinked it away. Useless. The implant had always been useless. A cage disguised as augmentation. Data disguised as wisdom. She'd volunteered for it in Miami, desperate for relief from grief, and all it did was show her more dying birds, more extinction data, more proof that nothing mattered.

Footsteps behind her. How close? She didn't turn.

A memory flashed: childhood game. "Owl and Mice." Standing in a glade with her cousins, waiting for the signal, then the rush as the predators streamed in from all sides. That electric terror-joy of being chased, knowing you'd probably be caught, running anyway because running was the point.

She was the mouse now.

And they were the owls.

Her implant glitched hard, overlaying vision with fractal patterns—faces in trees, eyes everywhere, the network's telepathic whisper: *we see you we see you we see you*

Or maybe she was hallucinating. Hypoxia. Adrenaline. Fear.

Did it matter?

She stumbled, caught herself on a tree trunk. Bark rough against her palm. Real. Solid. The forest smelled like pine and moss and her own fear-sweat. She could hear them behind her—not running, just walking. Steady. Inevitable. They weren't trying to catch her. They were herding her. Had been all along.

She pushed off the tree, kept running.

But something had shifted. The terror was gone. In its place: acceptance. Strange calm. She'd bought Mike time. That was enough. More than enough. She'd failed Iphy. Failed their marriage. Failed to stop James's grooming, failed to see the cult's manipulation. But this? This one thing she could do. Lead them away. Sacrifice herself for a man she'd once loved, once married, once destroyed and was destroyed by.

Ethical responsibility. Not romance. Not redemption.

Just: this is what you do when you've taken everything from someone. You give them a chance to escape.

The trees thinned ahead. Light breaking through. A clearing.

She burst through underbrush into open space.


SCENE 4: THE CLEARING / GOLDEN-WINGED WARBLERS

Dawn.

She hadn't noticed it arriving. But the sky was gray now, not black. Pre-sunrise. That liminal moment when nocturnal animals retreat and diurnal ones emerge. Shift change. The world's quiet breath before the day's scream.

The clearing was small. Maybe thirty feet across. Grass silver with dew. A fallen log, rotting, moss-covered. Trees ringing the space like an amphitheater.

She stood in the center, breathing hard, bleeding from a dozen small cuts, exhausted beyond language.

Behind her: footsteps entering the clearing. Slow. Deliberate.

She didn't turn around.

Instead she looked up.

And saw them.

Two small birds. Low branch. Gray and yellow and white. Unmistakable.

Golden-winged Warblers.

Male and female.

Her implant was silent. No data. No network. Just her own eyes, her own knowledge, the ornithologist's training that lived deeper than any augmentation.

The male was perfect. Yellow face and breast. Gray wings with two crisp white bars. Black throat patch and eye stripe. Breeding plumage. He was singing—that distinctive five-note song: *zee-zee-zee-zoo-zee*. Territorial call. Defending this tiny space from rivals that didn't exist anymore because there were no rivals left.

The female was beside him. Duller colors. Watching.

Last pair.

Persephone knew it in her bones. This was it. The last breeding pair of Golden-winged Warblers in Maine. Maybe the last in North America. Maybe the last anywhere.

Mike's research species. The bird he'd spent years studying, trying to save, documenting with desperate precision as its habitat disappeared under development and climate change and the ten thousand casual violences of human expansion.

And here they were.

Final witnesses.

The male sang again. That ridiculous five-note assertion of existence. *I am here. This is mine. I will breed. I will pass on my genes. I will continue.*

Except he wouldn't.

The math was irrefutable. One breeding pair can't sustain a population. They were functionally extinct. Still singing, still defending territory, still going through the motions of a species that had already ended but didn't know it yet.

Like humans.

Like her.

Persephone felt something break open in her chest. Not grief. Not exactly. Something older. Awe. Recognition. The male warbler and she were the same. Both performing behaviors that no longer mattered. Both clinging to meaning in a universe that had moved on.

She'd spent her career counting dead birds. Surveying dawn collisions in Miami. Documenting extinction in real-time. Bearing witness to the end of the world.

And now the world was witnessing her end.

Full circle.

A memory: Iphy seeing her first warbler. Five years old, in Quebec, during the summer research trip. Yellow warbler (not this species, a different one, commoner, safer). Iphy's face had lit up. "It's so small!" she'd said. "How does it fly so far?"

"I don't know," Persephone had answered. Honest. She didn't know. No one knew. Migration was compulsion, instinct, doom. The birds flew because they had to. Because their genes said fly. Even when it killed them.

The male warbler sang again.

*Zee-zee-zee-zoo-zee.*

Persephone listened. Really listened. Without implant mediation. Without data overlay. Just pure sound. Music.

It was beautiful.

That was the unbearable thing. It was beautiful. And it meant nothing. Beauty and meaninglessness weren't contradictions. They were the same thing.

The footsteps behind her stopped.

She could feel them now. The cult members. However many there were. Forming a circle. Closing in.

She didn't turn around.

The warblers stayed. Watching her. Or maybe not. Maybe they'd forgotten she existed. Maybe she was already gone.

The male sang one more time.

Then they flew.

Two small bodies arrowing into gray sky. Gray wings white bars yellow flash. Gone.

Persephone closed her eyes.


SCENE 5: THE SURROUNDING

When she opened them, they were there.

Forming a circle around the clearing. Ten? Fifteen? Twenty? She didn't count. Didn't matter.

She recognized some faces. The woman from the bonfire. The man with the apron. James's friends, maybe. Townspeople. Or maybe she was wrong. Maybe these were strangers. Maybe they'd been following her since Miami, since the surgery, since before she even knew there was a network to be part of.

They didn't speak.

Just stood. Watching.

Her implant was completely dead now. No static. No glitching. No network whisper. Just silence. She was fully human again. Unaugmented. Alone inside her own skull for the first time in months.

It felt like suffocation.

Or freedom.

She couldn't tell the difference anymore.

She stood in the center of the circle and waited. For what? Violence? Ritual? Explanation? None came. They just watched. Like she'd watched dying birds in Miami. Like the warblers had watched her. Witness. That was all. The universe's only ethic: see and be seen.

Her legs gave out.

She didn't fall—just sank slowly to her knees in the wet grass. Exhaustion. Blood loss. Something deeper. The strings cut. Puppet collapsing.

The cultists didn't move closer.

Maybe they didn't need to.

Maybe this was always how it ended. Not with violence but with inevitability. The prey stops running. The predator stops chasing. The dance concludes.

She thought about Mike. Hoped he'd made it to the road. Hoped he'd find help, find safety. Hoped. Even though hope was another lie, another human narcissism, she hoped anyway.

She thought about Iphy. Yellow dress floating in pool water. Small body. Too late. Always too late.

She thought about James. His hands, tender and violating. His ideology, maybe right, definitely wrong. Dead now. Blood on someone else's hands.

She thought about the implant. The violation of her skull. The promise of clarity that delivered only more data, more despair, more proof that consciousness was just meat processing inputs until the meat stopped.

She thought about the warblers. Their song. Meaningless and beautiful.

The cold grass soaked through her jeans.

The cultists watched.

Somewhere, a crow cawed.

Her vision blurred. Hypoxia? Shock? Or something else—boundaries dissolving, self dispersing, the illusion of "I" finally fragmenting into the trillion cellular truths it always was.

She closed her eyes again.

And this time, when she opened them, she wasn't looking out anymore.

She was looking in.
