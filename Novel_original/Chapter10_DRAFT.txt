Chapter 10: Ares Down

Day 22. She emerged from the forest like Orpheus climbing from Hades—changed, diminished, carrying the weight of what she'd seen. The hermitage was over. James had come to collect her, saying nothing, just packing her gear while she stood motionless, a woman-shaped void. The tent collapsed. The transects abandoned. The data corrupted or irrelevant or both.

They drove back to town in silence.

She watched the trees pass—hemlock, ash, maple, birch—and saw them all dying. Not future-tense. Present continuous. The adelgid burrowing, the fungus spreading, the climate shifting, each tree a slow-motion catastrophe pretending to be landscape. Beautiful corpses standing upright. Wooden gravestones marking their own graves.

The implant whispered metrics she didn't want: *Tsuga canadensis. Mortality rate: 94%. Ecosystem collapse: 18 months.* She blinked it away. It returned. You couldn't shut off knowing once you'd learned how to see.

James glanced at her. Said nothing. What could he say? She'd gone mad in the woods and he'd let it happen—watched through the network, reported to the coven, documented her dissolution like a thesis supervisor monitoring a failing PhD student. Complicit. Caring. Both true simultaneously. She hated him. She needed him. The contradictions didn't resolve.

They arrived at the farmhouse as the sun angled toward late afternoon. Golden light, elegiac, the kind of beauty that only existed because everything was ending. If the world were eternal, light wouldn't matter. Mortality made it precious. She hated that too—the sentimentality of doom, the way apocalypse bred aesthetics.

"There's something tonight," James said finally, killing the engine. "At the bar in town."

She looked at him.

"The Ares I launch. They're showing it on the big screen. Everyone's going."

Persephone's chest tightened. *Mike.* She hadn't thought about Mike in weeks, hadn't let herself. Her ex-husband—no, almost-ex-husband, divorce not finalized, just separation, just mutual retreat from the wreckage of Iphy's drowning. He'd been training for Ares I. Aerospace engineer. Mission specialist. Going to Mars because Earth was intolerable, because their marriage was intolerable, because everywhere he looked he saw Iphy's empty bedroom and her mother's accusing eyes.

"Is he—" Her voice cracked. "Is Mike on that mission?"

James hesitated. "I don't know. I thought... maybe you'd want to check?"

She didn't move. Couldn't. Some part of her had assumed Mike was still on Earth, still in Houston, still orbiting the trauma of their daughter's death like a moon orbiting a dead planet. But of course he'd gone. Of course he'd left. What else was space but the ultimate escape? Sixty million kilometers from grief. From guilt. From her.

"Okay," she whispered. "I'll come."

---

The bar was called The Brass Lantern, a name that promised rustic charm and delivered exactly that: exposed beams, Edison bulbs, chalkboard menus with locally-sourced lies. It was packed. Bodies everywhere, voices loud, the air thick with beer and sweat and excitement. Humanity at its most performative—gathering to witness transcendence, to cheer the rocket that would carry them vicariously off this dying rock, if only for an evening.

Persephone and James pushed inside. Found space at the bar. He ordered whiskey for both. She drank without tasting, the burn irrelevant, just chemical warmth spreading through a body that felt increasingly not-hers.

The television dominated the far wall—massive flat screen, high definition, NASA feed streaming live from Cape Canaveral. The Ares I rocket stood on the pad, white and enormous, exhaling vapor like a sleeping dragon. Countdown clock: T-minus 37 minutes.

"Beautiful, isn't it?" someone said nearby. A woman with graying hair, eyes wet with emotion. "First Mars mission in fifteen years. We're going back."

Persephone didn't respond. Stared at the rocket. It looked like a monument—not to future, to past. To the Apollo era, to Cold War optimism, to the delusion that fleeing Earth would save anything. As if Mars weren't just another graveyard, colder and redder, waiting for the same species to bring the same failures to new soil.

Her implant activated without prompting: *Ares I Mission Specs: Crew of 8. Duration: 3 years round trip. Payload: 47 metric tons. Success probability: 68%. Mortality risk: 32%. Cost: $940 billion USD. Earth species driven extinct during mission development: 1,847.*

She closed her eyes. The numbers burned anyway.

"You okay?" James's hand on her shoulder.

"No."

"Want to leave?"

"No."

She drank. He drank. The crowd roared at something—some commentator on TV, some platitude about human spirit and frontier courage. The usual liturgy. Manifest Destiny in a spacesuit. Colonialism with better PR.

The countdown ticked. T-minus 20 minutes.

Persephone searched the screen for crew photos. There—eight faces smiling in orange flight suits, posing in front of an American flag. She scanned them desperately, looking for Mike's face, his dark hair, his engineer's serious expression—

He wasn't there.

Relief and confusion warred. She pulled out her phone—first time in days. Searched news. Found it immediately:

**ARES I CREW SHAKEUP: LAST-MINUTE REPLACEMENT AFTER MEDICAL DISQUALIFICATION**

She read faster. Mike's name. Removed from manifest two weeks before launch. "Psychological evaluation concerns." Replacement crew member: Dr. Sarah Chen, propulsion engineer.

Persephone's hands trembled. *Psychological concerns.* She knew exactly what that meant. Grief. PTSD. The same thing eating her alive, eating him too, but his job required clearance, required mental fitness, required the performance of stability. He'd failed the test. They'd cut him.

She could see it so clearly: Mike in Houston, packing his apartment, watching his replacements board the rocket he'd trained for, the future he'd escaped toward suddenly closed. Trapped on Earth with the rest of them. With her.

"They replaced him," she whispered.

James leaned closer. "What?"

"Mike. He's not on the mission. They cut him."

James said nothing. What could he say?

The countdown continued. T-minus 10 minutes. The bar noise swelled, people ordering final drinks before the show, the spectacle, the bright expenditure of a billion dollars to fling eight bodies toward a red desert that would kill them slowly if the rocket didn't kill them fast.

Persephone felt it building—not anxiety, something colder. The cosmic absurdity of it. All this effort, all this hope, all this technology marshaled toward escape, and for what? So eight people could plant a flag and collect rocks and transmit data back to a planet that wouldn't exist long enough to do anything with it? Mars missions took three years. At current warming rates, Earth would cross irreversible tipping points before they returned. They'd come home to find the Anthropocene in full collapse—coasts submerged, forests burning, crop failures cascading, the political order fragmenting into climate wars and refugee crises.

*There is no escape,* she thought. *The rocket is a monument to delusion. We're trapped here. All of us. And we're taking everything with us when we go.*

Her implant flickered: **[Philosophical insight detected. Match found: Accelerationism. Theorist: Nick Land. Quote archived.]**

The quote appeared unbidden across her vision:

*"It's the impossibility of stopping escape. But the escape is not for us. It's the escape from us."*

She laughed—sharp, bitter sound lost in bar noise. Yes. Exactly. The rocket wasn't for humans. It was for whatever came after. AI, uploaded consciousness, post-biological intelligence—something that could survive the void humans couldn't. Ares was a relay station. A stepping stone. Humanity burning itself as fuel for the next iteration.

"Another," she said to the bartender, pushing her empty glass forward.

James watched her but didn't intervene. Maybe he understood. Maybe he'd already seen this trajectory—Persephone accelerating toward her own event horizon, the singularity of grief where meaning collapsed into itself and vanished.

T-minus 5 minutes.

The bar hushed. Even the cynics quieted. Something about rocket launches silenced the species—residual primate awe at fire, at flight, at things heavier than air defying gravity. Icarus complex. Prometheus complex. We couldn't stop reaching for things that would destroy us.

The NASA feed showed closeups: fuel lines detaching, service towers retracting, vapor venting from the rocket's skin. It looked alive. A creature preparing to molt. To shed Earth like dead skin and rise toward something purer.

*Nothing is pure,* Persephone thought. *The rocket is made from rare earth minerals strip-mined from indigenous land. The fuel refined from petroleum extracted through ecological genocide. The computer chips built in factories where workers jumped from roofs. We pretend this is transcendence but it's just violence hidden behind engineering.*

T-minus 1 minute.

Someone started chanting: "Ten! Nine! Eight!"

The bar joined: "Seven! Six! Five!"

Persephone didn't chant. Drank instead. James beside her, silent, his hand finding hers under the bar. She let him hold it. Physical anchor. Last human contact before the symbolic launch carried them all somewhere irreversible.

"Four! Three! Two! One!"

The rocket ignited.

Fire bloomed at the base—orange, white, impossible brightness. The Ares I lifted slowly, fighting gravity, engines screaming, nine million pounds of thrust tearing it free from Earth. It rose. Faster. The bar erupted—cheers, applause, someone crying, everyone watching the white streak climb through blue Florida sky toward the black.

Persephone watched the fire. Not the rocket. The exhaust. The burned fuel falling back to Earth, the expenditure, the waste. Bataille's solar economy made literal—energy spent for no purpose except the spending itself. The rocket wasn't going anywhere that mattered. It was just burning. Humanity's signature move: accelerate toward nothing, expending everything, call it progress.

The rocket climbed. Stage one separation. More fire. More cheers.

James squeezed her hand. "You okay?"

"No," she said. "Let's get out of here."

---

Outside, the afternoon had aged into early evening. The sun hung low, gold turning orange, the same orange as rocket exhaust, as fire, as the horizon on the day Iphy drowned. Everything was fire. Everything burning slowly. The only question was whether you noticed.

They walked without direction. Away from the bar, away from the crowds spilling onto the sidewalk to smoke and celebrate. The town was small—two blocks of shops, a gas station, a church with a white steeple like a bone pointing at heaven. Persephone felt outside of it. Ghost walking through a world that believed in itself, in futures, in the possibility of Tuesdays and Thursdays and grocery lists.

"Where are we going?" James asked.

"Anywhere."

They walked. Out of town, onto a dirt road, past farmhouses with lights glowing warm, past fields gone fallow, past a burned barn standing like a ribcage. The sun sank. Orange deepened to red. The sky looked apocalyptic—clouds lit from beneath, the whole atmosphere on fire.

"I keep thinking about the stars," Persephone said suddenly.

James glanced at her. Waited.

"Everyone romanticizes them. The stars. Billions of lights, so beautiful, so full of possibility. But what no one talks about is the void. The black between them." She stopped walking, looked up at the sky—still too bright for stars, just empty blue darkening to purple. "The stars are sparse islands of fusion separated by incomprehensible vastness. Cold. Dark. Silent. If there are civilizations out there, they're screaming into that void the same way we are. Alone. Deaf to each other. By the time we see their light, they're already dead. Already extinct. We're looking at graveyards and calling them heavens."

James was quiet for a long time. Then: "That's bleak."

"It's true."

"Maybe. But does truth have to be bleak?"

She looked at him. His face orange in dying light, earnest, still trying to find meaning. Still infected with hope. She wanted to shake him. Wanted to kiss him. Wanted to dissolve into him and stop thinking, stop knowing, stop carrying the weight of pattern recognition that only revealed horror.

"Yes," she said finally. "Truth is bleak. Everything else is coping."

He smiled sadly. "Then I guess I'm coping."

They kept walking. The road curved through a stand of pines, then opened onto a long fallow field—acres of grass gone wild, golden in sunset, beautiful in the way abandoned things are beautiful. A barn sagged at the far edge. No house. No people. Just land returning to itself, pretending civilization had never happened.

"Wait," James said, stopping.

Persephone turned. He was looking at his phone, face illuminated by screen glow.

"What?"

He didn't answer immediately. Scrolled. His expression shifted—confusion, then alarm.

"James?"

"The rocket." His voice strange. "Something's wrong."

Her stomach dropped. "What do you mean?"

He turned the phone toward her. News alert: **ARES I MISSION: ANOMALY DETECTED DURING STAGE TWO BURN.**

She grabbed the phone. Read faster. Telemetry loss. Communication blackout. NASA scrambling. No visual confirmation. Standby for updates.

They looked at each other.

"We need to get back," James said.

But Persephone was already walking—not back toward town, toward the bar and the screens and the crowds. Toward the field. Away. Deeper into the orange light, the fallow grass, the place where no one was watching anything except sky.

"Persephone—"

"I can't," she said without turning. "I can't go back there. I can't watch it on TV with commentators and speculation and—"

She stopped. Looked up. The sky was darkening fast, stars beginning to pierce through. Somewhere up there, sixty miles high or more, the Ares I was burning or breaking or already gone. Eight people. Sarah Chen who'd replaced Mike. Seven others with families, children, futures that might already be past-tense.

James caught up. Stood beside her. Put his phone away. They stood together in the field as the sun died and the stars emerged—cold, distant, indifferent.

"If it exploded," Persephone said quietly, "Mike is alive. And he'll know he should've been on it."

James said nothing.

"Survivor's guilt on top of everything else. Iphy's death. The career ending. Now this." She laughed—harsh, broken sound. "He's going to destroy himself."

"Maybe he'll—"

"Don't." She cut him off. "Don't say maybe. Don't give me hope. I can't carry hope anymore. It's too heavy."

Silence. A crow called in the distance. The field grass rustled.

James touched her arm. Gently. "Then let's not carry anything tonight."

She looked at him. His face in twilight, half-shadow, half-remaining light. He meant it as comfort. As escape. The same thing the rocket meant, the same thing the alcohol meant, the same thing every distraction meant—stop thinking, stop hurting, just exist in body instead of mind.

"Okay," she whispered.

---

They walked deeper into the field. The grass rose to their waists, seed heads catching on their clothes. The barn loomed ahead, half-collapsed, roof caved on one side. They passed it. Found a clearing beyond—small rise, grass shorter, flattened by deer maybe. The ground sloped gently. You could see the horizon from here, the last red line of sun bleeding into purple-black.

James spread his jacket on the ground. Gestured. She sat. He sat beside her. They didn't speak. Just watched the light drain from the world, watched the stars multiply, the Milky Way beginning to show—pale river of ancient light, each photon travelling centuries to reach eyes that might not exist tomorrow.

"Do you think they're dead?" James asked finally.

"Yes."

"You don't know that."

"I know probabilities. I know engineering. I know what 'anomaly' means when NASA stops transmitting."

He nodded slowly. Accepted it. They sat with the knowledge—eight people gone, maybe, probably, almost certainly. Turned to vapor and debris, atoms scattered across the mesosphere, never reaching Mars, never reaching anywhere. The rocket a coffin accelerating into void.

"Does it matter?" Persephone said. "If they died today or forty years from now in nursing homes. Does the timing matter?"

"Of course it matters."

"Why?"

James struggled. "Because... those forty years are life. Experience. Love. Meaning."

"Meaning." She tasted the word. "What meaning? We're biological machines producing the illusion of significance to cope with mortality. The universe doesn't care if you live forty years or forty seconds. The outcome is identical: heat death, entropy, void."

"Then why are we here?" His voice rising, frustrated. "Why are you still looking for the warbler? Why document anything? Why care?"

She didn't answer immediately. Stared at the stars—the vast cold dark between them, the nothing that was most of everything.

"Because I can't stop," she said finally. "It's not hope. It's compulsion. Like the migration. Birds fly thousands of miles toward breeding grounds that might not exist anymore. They can't not fly. They're bound by pattern, by instinct, by the weight of evolutionary history. I'm the same. I look for warblers because that's what ornithologists do. Even when it's futile. Especially when it's futile. The futility is the point. It's all we have—the dignity of continuing when there's no reason to continue."

James was quiet. Then: "That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"It's true."

"Stop saying that."

"Stop asking me to lie."

He turned to her suddenly, grabbed her shoulders. Not rough—desperate. "I'm not asking you to lie. I'm asking you to feel something other than despair. Just for one night. Just—" His voice cracked. "I can't watch you disappear."

She looked at his face. Saw the fear there, the care, the love that couldn't save anything. He was drowning too. They all were. The forest, the market, the rocket, the planet—everything sinking slowly, together, alone.

"Okay," she whispered. "What do you want?"

"I don't know. Something real. Something not... pattern. Not data. Not doom."

She understood. Bataille understood. Georges Bataille writing about eroticism and death, about expenditure and excess, about the way humans tried to escape the trap of selfhood through sex, through violence, through any dissolution of boundary. The orgasm as little death. The rocket launch as cosmic orgasm. Humanity spending itself toward nothing, gloriously, wastefully, because the spending was all that mattered.

She kissed him.

Not gentle. Desperate. Mouth hard against his, hands pulling him closer, body asserting its claims against the void. If thought was doom then stop thinking. If consciousness was prison then escape into flesh. The animal solution. The only solution that predated language, predated civilization, predated the terrible knowledge that separated humans from the rest of the kingdom Animalia.

James responded—surprise giving way to hunger. His hands in her hair, her hands tugging his shirt free, buttons irrelevant, just fabric between skin and they needed skin, needed heat, needed to feel alive while the rocket burned and the stars watched and the Earth turned indifferent beneath them.

They fell back onto the jacket. Grass beneath it, cold ground, but bodies generated warmth, friction, the fever of need. Clothes shed—her shirt gone, his pants open, her jeans kicked free. The evening air cold against exposed skin but they didn't care, just pressed closer, giving heat to each other, mouths tasting salt and whiskey and desperation.

Above them: stars. Billions. Dead light from dead civilizations, if Land was right, if the accelerationists were right, if the Fermi Paradox resolved the way Persephone thought it did—every intelligence reaching the same threshold and failing, burning out, leaving only ruins and radio silence. Humanity wasn't special. Would reach the same limit. Was reaching it now. The rocket exploding was just preview, just rehearsal for the species-wide immolation.

But here, now, in the grass: warmth. Bodies moving. James above her, face lost in shadow, hands braced on ground beside her head. She wrapped her legs around him, pulled him closer, felt him enter her—the oldest transaction, the oldest ritual, two bodies briefly merging, briefly forgetting they were separate things destined for separate graves.

The pleasure built slowly. She let it. Didn't think. Just felt—skin, pressure, rhythm, the physical present tense that required no future, no past, just the immediate now. Her breathing synced with his. Hearts racing. Sweat despite the cold. The implant tried to activate—tried to measure heart rate, dopamine, oxytocin—but she shut it down, refused the data, just wanted to feel without knowing, just once.

James moved faster. She matched him. The grass rustled. The field breathed around them. Somewhere above, the Ares mission was debris or miracle, exploded or saved, but it didn't matter here, didn't touch this small pocket of animal warmth in a cold universe.

She thought of Mike. Couldn't help it. Mike in Houston, watching the same sky, knowing his replacement crew was dead or dying, knowing he should've been with them, knowing survival was just delayed death. She thought of Iphy. Iphy in the pool, drowning, lungs filling with chlorinated water while her parents fought inside about something irrelevant, something that evaporated the moment they found her floating. She thought of the warblers. Thought of the forest. Thought of the market and the pig and the children eating ice cream while the planet burned.

Then she thought nothing. The pleasure peaked—white flash behind her eyes, her body arching, breath stopping, the small death that mimicked the large one, consciousness dissolving briefly into pure sensation. She heard herself cry out—animal sound, no words, just release.

James followed—his rhythm breaking, body tensing, then collapsing against her, both of them panting, hearts pounding, alive alive alive in the aftermath.

They lay tangled together. His weight on her, her arms around him. The stars wheeled overhead. Time passed—seconds or minutes, irrelevant.

Then James's phone buzzed. Once. Twice. Insistent.

He pulled away reluctantly. Reached for his pants, found the phone. The screen lit his face blue-white. His expression changed.

"What?" Persephone asked, though she already knew.

He turned the phone toward her.

News alert. All caps: **ARES I MISSION LOST. EXPLOSION CONFIRMED. NO SURVIVORS.**

The words hung there. She read them three times. They didn't change.

Eight people. Gone. Sarah Chen who'd taken Mike's place. Seven others whose names she didn't know, whose children would grow up without parents, whose parents would bury empty caskets or scatter ashes or just stare at the sky.

Persephone started laughing.

"Don't," James said quietly.

But she couldn't stop. Laughter bubbling up from the same place the orgasm had come from, the same place the screaming in the forest had come from—the place where horror and absurdity fused, where meaning collapsed into itself and became farce. They'd just fucked in a field while eight people burned alive sixty miles up. They'd spent themselves—biological expenditure, erotic waste—while the rocket spent its fuel and its crew and a billion dollars in a bright orange flash. The symmetry was perfect. The Bataillean logic complete. Everything was fire. Everything spent itself. Sex and death and rocket launches—all the same thing, all brief light against infinite darkness.

"Persephone—" James tried to hold her.

She pushed him away. Sat up. Grass stuck to her bare skin, clothes scattered around her like debris. She looked up at the stars—those cold dead lights, those billion graveyards.

"We're next," she said. "You know that, right? The rocket was rehearsal. Mars was rehearsal. This whole thing—civilization, progress, meaning—it's all rehearsal for the same ending. Explosion or heat death or climate collapse, doesn't matter. The outcome is identical."

"Stop."

"We fucked while they burned, James. We felt pleasure while eight people turned into vapor. What does that make us?"

"Human."

She laughed again—bitter, broken. "Yes. Exactly. Human. The species that watches catastrophe and gets aroused. That builds monuments to escape and fills them with corpses. That burns everything and calls it light."

James said nothing. Couldn't refute her. They sat in the grass, half-naked, the afterglow curdling into shame or clarity or both. The stars watched. The field watched. The Earth turned, carrying them eastward into night, into whatever came after orgasm and explosion and the last orange light draining from the sky.

Persephone stood. Started dressing. Mechanically—jeans, shirt, jacket. Her body felt distant again, meat-prison reasserting itself after brief dissolution. James dressed too. They didn't speak.

When they were clothed, when the evidence of what they'd done was hidden beneath fabric and normalcy, James asked: "What now?"

Persephone looked toward town. Lights glowing in the distance. The bar probably erupting, crowds processing the news, mourning or not mourning, humanity's famous resilience already beginning its work—tragedy into story, story into myth, myth into background radiation.

"Now Mike comes," she said quietly. "He'll have seen the news. He'll know I'm here. He'll come looking for me."

"Why?"

"Because I'm all he has left." She turned to James. "Iphy is dead. His career is over. His replacement crew is gone. I'm the last thread connecting him to the world. And threads pull."

James looked afraid. Of Mike? Of her? Of the pattern tightening around them all, the spiral accelerating toward conclusion, toward the ending that had been written from the beginning?

"What will you do when he comes?"

Persephone didn't answer. Didn't know. Started walking back toward the road, toward town, toward whatever came next. James followed. Above them the stars multiplied—cold, infinite, separated by gulfs no rocket would cross, no species would bridge. Alone in the dark. Screaming at the deaf heavens. The human condition rendered in vacuum and distant light.

Behind them, in the field where they'd joined and separated, the grass slowly straightened. Erasing evidence. Nature patient with human drama. The ground would reclaim everything eventually—the jacket, the barn, the town, the species. Entropy always won. Always. The rocket explosion proved it. The orgasm proved it. Bataille understood: we were all just brief expenditure against inevitable cold.

The only question was whether the spending mattered.

Persephone walked, and didn't know, and kept walking anyway.

---

By the time they reached the farmhouse, it was full dark. No moon—new moon phase, the sky black except for stars. James unlocked the door. They entered. The house felt different—charged, expectant, like a stage set waiting for actors.

Persephone went to the window. Looked out at the forest. Somewhere in those trees, the coven was watching through her implant, through the network, through mycelial threads and panpsychist connection. They'd felt her orgasm. They'd felt the rocket explode. They knew Mike was coming. They knew everything ended in chapter twelve, chapter thirteen, the climax written into pattern before she'd ever arrived.

"Are you okay?" James asked from behind her.

"No."

"Do you want tea? Food? I can—"

"I want to be alone."

Silence. Then his footsteps retreating. Door closing. She was alone with the stars and the forest and the implant humming at her temples, documenting her despair, feeding it to the spiral, the great pattern that consumed everything and revealed nothing.

She thought of Mike. Somewhere on a highway, driving north, radio playing news of the disaster, his hands white-knuckled on the wheel. Agamemnon returning from Troy. Returning from wars lost before they began. She should warn him. Should tell him to stay away, that nothing here would help, that she was drowning and couldn't save anyone.

But she didn't text. Didn't call. Let the pattern complete itself. Let the spiral turn.

Outside, the forest breathed. The stars burned cold. The Earth accelerated through space at 67,000 miles per hour, a rock hurtling through void, carrying eight billion passengers who didn't know they were moving, didn't know they were falling, didn't know the destination was written in thermodynamics and time.

Persephone stood at the window until the stars blurred. Not crying. Just tired. So tired. The exhaustion of pattern recognition, of seeing too clearly, of carrying knowledge that couldn't be unlearned.

The rocket had exploded. Mike was coming. The coven was waiting. Iphy was dead. The warblers were dying. The planet was dying. Everything was dying at different speeds, and she was dying too, consciousness slowly dissolving into the network, self becoming data, Persephone becoming pattern.

She whispered to her reflection in the window:

"Let it burn."

Bataille's answer. Land's answer. The only honest answer to a universe defined by expenditure and entropy. Don't fight the fire. Become it. Accelerate into annihilation. Let the spending be total, glorious, complete.

The implant flickered agreement: **[Philosophical alignment confirmed. Accelerationist threshold approaching. Prepare for transition.]**

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, the forest seemed closer. The walls thinner. The boundary between inside and outside dissolving. Chapter ten ending. Chapter eleven approaching. The market. The breakdown. The world revealing itself as ossuary, graveyard, mass extinction event disguised as normal life.

She was ready.

She had no choice.

The pattern had already written the ending.

She just had to live it now.
