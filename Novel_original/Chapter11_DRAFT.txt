Ch11 World Ossuary

SCENE 1: THE INVITATION

She hadn't moved from the couch in days. How many? Three? Four? Time had become viscous, pooling around her like the amber light through the farmhouse windows. Dust motes spiraled in columns of sun and she watched them fall, fall, always falling, never reaching bottom.

James entered with coffee. The smell reached her first—burnt, bitter, alive. He sat beside her, the couch sinking under his weight, tilting her body toward him like water seeking level.

"I thought we could go into town today," he said. Soft voice. Careful. Like approaching a spooked animal. "Market's open. Get some air."

She didn't respond. Couldn't. The words were sounds, phonemes without meaning, just air shaped by meat.

His hand found hers. Warm. Too warm. She could feel his pulse through his palm, blood pressure rising, arteries dilating, the hydraulic pump of his heart pushing fluid through miles of vessels. Alive alive alive. For now.

"Persephone." Her name in his mouth. "When's the last time you ate something I didn't bring you?"

"I'm not hungry."

"That's not what I asked."

Silence stretched between them like taffy. Outside, a crow cawed. The implant stuttered, overlaying data across her vision: *Corvus brachyrhynchos. STATUS: Viable. PROJECTED LIFESPAN: 4.2 years remaining. COGNITIVE CAPACITY: Tool use detected. SELF-AWARENESS: Probable.*

She blinked it away. The crow cawed again. Laughing. They were always laughing.

"Okay," she whispered. Not because she wanted to go. Because she was too tired to refuse. Because James's eyes held something she couldn't name—hope? Fear? Guilt? All three braided together like DNA strands, spiraling, replicating, doomed.

He smiled. It didn't reach his eyes.


SCENE 2: ARRIVAL AT MARKET

The truck smelled like motor oil and pine sap. James drove and she stared out the window at the passing trees—skeletal, leafless, waiting for a spring that would come too late or not at all. Ash trees, dying. Hemlocks, dying. The forest was a cemetery standing upright, pretending.

The implant whispered: *Tsuga canadensis. INFESTATION: Adelges tsugae confirmed. MORTALITY RATE: 94% within 20 years. ECOSYSTEM COLLAPSE: Imminent.*

"Stop," she whispered.

"What?"

"Nothing." Everything.

They arrived at the market in a flood of color. Bunting strung between poles, red white blue, cheerful lies. Folk music keened from speakers—mandolin, fiddle, someone singing about home. Children shrieked with laughter. Dogs wagged. The sun shone with obscene brightness.

James helped her out of the truck. Her legs had forgotten how to hold weight. When did she last walk? She leaned against him and he became architecture, load-bearing, necessary.

"You okay?"

No. "Yes."

They walked toward the entrance. Too many people. Too many voices braiding into a singular roar, white noise, ocean surf made of human need. Her implant flickered: *TARGET COUNT: 47 Homo sapiens. STATUS: Viable. AVERAGE LIFESPAN REMAINING: 31.4 years. MORTALITY RATE: 100%. COGNITIVE DISSONANCE: Detected.*

She stopped walking.

"Persephone?"

The market sprawled before her. Stalls arranged in rows. Vendors calling out. Families browsing. Children eating cotton candy, ice cream melting down their wrists. Normal. This was supposed to be normal. This was how humans gathered, traded, fed themselves. Civilization. Ten thousand years of agriculture leading to this—bunting and mandolins and vendors shouting about heirloom tomatoes.

But she saw beneath it. The structure dissolving, revealing bones.

"Come on," James said gently, his hand on her elbow, steering her forward into the mouth of the beast.


SCENE 3: FIRST BREAKDOWN — VEGETABLE STALLS

The first stall sold produce. Mountains of it—tomatoes, squash, carrots, lettuce arranged in wooden crates like offerings at an altar. The vendor, a woman with sun-weathered skin and a kind smile, held up a tomato. Red. Perfect. Dying.

"First of the season!" she announced proudly.

Persephone stared at the tomato. The implant scanned: *Solanum lycopersicum. TIME SINCE HARVEST: 14 hours. CELLULAR DEGRADATION: 8%. ESTIMATED TIME TO DECOMPOSITION: 72 hours. ETHYLENE PRODUCTION: Accelerating. STATUS: Dying but unaware.*

She reached out. Touched it. Felt—

Roots cut. Stem bleeding. Chloroplasts starving, photosynthesis stopped, mitochondria gasping for oxygen they'd never receive. The tomato was still alive, cells firing electrical signals, screaming silently into the void, and no one heard, no one cared, because it was just a vegetable, just food, just—

"Beautiful, right?" The vendor beamed. "Organic. No pesticides. We grew them ourselves."

Persephone's hand trembled against the tomato's skin. She could feel it dying beneath her palm. Not metaphor. Not imagination. The panpsychic connection opened in Chapter 9 had become a wound and everything poured in—plant death, cell death, the small apocalypse of harvest multiplied across every crate, every stall, every basket.

"Persephone?" James's voice from far away. "You okay?"

She pulled her hand back like touching fire. Stumbled away from the stall. But there were more—

Squash. Carrots. Lettuce. All screaming. All dying.

The implant catalogued relentlessly: *Cucurbita pepo. Root system severed. Mycorrhizal network terminated. Loneliness detected.*

*Daucus carota. Cambium cells failing. Offspring abandoned in soil. Lineage terminated.*

*Lactuca sativa. Chloroplasts attempting photosynthesis. Failure. Failure. Failure.*

Her knees weakened. James caught her arm.

"Let's… let's move along," he said, uncertain now, worry creasing his forehead.

But there was nowhere to move. Everywhere she looked: death pretending to be life. Corpses stacked in baskets and called fresh. The dead feeding the living who were just the dead-in-waiting.

*Green*, she thought wildly. *So much green. Chlorophyll. Life. No—death pretending to be life. Photosynthesis stopped. Mitochondria failing. We stack corpses in baskets and call them fresh and hand over money and smile and say thank you and go home and we—*

"Breathe," James whispered. "Just breathe."

But breathing was violence too. Oxygen molecules tearing through her lungs, oxidizing cells, aging her with every inhalation. Respiration was just slow burning. She was on fire and so was everyone else and no one noticed because the flames were invisible.


SCENE 4: SECOND BREAKDOWN — MEAT & FISH

James tried steering her away, toward the craft stalls—pottery, textiles, things that were already dead and done dying. But they had to pass the butcher's tent and she made the mistake of looking.

The pig hung from a hook. Headless. Split down the center. Pink meat gleaming under fluorescent buzz. Beautiful, in the way catastrophes are beautiful.

Her implant locked on: *TARGET IDENTIFIED: Sus scrofa domesticus. STATUS: Deceased. CAUSE OF DEATH: Mechanical exsanguination. ESTIMATED AGE AT DEATH: 6 months. NATURAL LIFESPAN: 15-20 years. EFFICIENCY RATIO: 4.1% lifespan utilized. REPRODUCTIVE STATUS: Pregnant. OFFSPRING TERMINATED: 10.*

The world lurched.

She heard it. Not with ears but with the terrible empathy the implant had unlocked—cellular memory encoded in muscle tissue. Squealing. The pig's final moments recorded in flesh, terror written in blood cells, the heart's last desperate beats echoing through dead chambers. And underneath, quieter, the embryonic pigs that never drew breath, ten potential futures snuffed before ignition.

*Pregnant*, the implant repeated helpfully. *Sow. Mother. Maternal pheromones detected in adipose tissue. Distress markers confirmed.*

"No," Persephone whispered.

"What?" James turned, saw where she was looking. "Oh. Let's—"

But she'd already seen the fish.

Salmon, trout, mackerel piled on ice, mouths frozen in death-gasps, eyes clouded like dirty glass. The implant reached back through time: *Devonian Period. 375 million years. Tiktaalik. Lobe-finned fish. Terrestrial transition. Ancestor identified.*

We came from you. Climbed onto land with your fins-becoming-legs, survived the great extinctions, radiated across continents, built cities and temples and particle accelerators, and now we stack your descendants on ice and charge eighteen dollars a pound.

The fish were suffocating. Past-tense suffocating, death already complete, but she felt it anyway—gills collapsing, blood oxygen plummeting, the slow drowning of creatures made to breathe water dying in air while humans breathed air and pretended they were different, distinct, separate from the kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, family: *our family*. We're eating our cousins.

The chicken carcasses watched her with empty sockets. *Gallus gallus domesticus. Descendant of jungle fowl. 50 billion slaughtered annually. Passenger Pigeon: extinct. Carolina Parakeet: extinct. Great Auk: extinct. This market is a mass extinction event in miniature. This is what we do. This is what we are.*

Her legs gave out.

She fell to her knees in the dirt, hyperventilating, the world spinning, and—

Laughing.

She couldn't stop laughing. Manic, broken laughter bubbling up from some deep place where horror and absurdity fused into a single thing. The pig died pregnant and someone bought ribs for $4.99 per pound. Ares exploded and people ate hot dogs. Iphy drowned and the world kept turning, kept eating, kept slaughtering, kept stacking corpses in refrigerated cases and calling it civilization.

"Persephone—" James knelt beside her, hands on her shoulders. "Look at me—"

She looked. Saw him dying too. Bones beneath skin, organs slowly failing, cells accumulating mutations, telomeres fraying with each division. Mortal. Temporary. Meat puppet animated by electrical signals that would stop, someday soon, and he'd be hung from a different kind of hook, lowered into earth or burned to ash, and either way the outcome was the same: dissolution.

She laughed harder. Couldn't breathe. Couldn't stop.

People gathered. Concerned murmurs. *Is she okay? Should we call someone? What's wrong with her?*

What's wrong? Everything. Nothing. The same thing that's always been wrong—we're conscious meat on a dying planet and we pretend we're not, we buy tomatoes and chicken carcasses and smile and say thank you and go home and watch TV and make love and have children who we name and cherish and who will also die, someday, after watching their own children die or before, in accidents or drownings or rocket explosions, and none of it means anything, it's just biology, just chemistry, just the universe doing what it does, converting order to entropy, life to death, structure to chaos, and—

James lifted her. Bodily. She'd gone limp, dissociated, eyes open but not seeing. He carried her through the crowd like a bride or a corpse—both thresholds, both crossings, both endings.

"Is she alright?" the vendor called.

James didn't answer. Focused on extraction. Getting her out of the market, away from the stalls, the carcasses, the cheerful apocalypse of normalcy.


SCENE 5: THIRD BREAKDOWN — THE CROWD

The truck. He got her to the truck. Set her in the passenger seat, brought water from the cab. She didn't drink. Just stared past him, back at the market.

From this distance she could see them all: *people eating*.

A child with an ice cream cone, vanilla dripping down his chin. Licking. Consuming. Milk extracted from cows bred for overproduction, their calves taken at birth, the mothers calling for weeks. But the ice cream was sweet. The child was happy.

A man bit into a hot dog. Teeth tearing casing, processed meat sliding down his throat, breaking down into proteins into amino acids into cellular fuel. Meat eating meat.

A couple shared fried dough, sugar and oil, empty calories, slow poison. They laughed. They were alive. They were dying. Both true simultaneously.

Her implant whispered: *Consumption pattern detected. Caloric intake: excessive. Nutrient value: minimal. Dopamine response: confirmed. Addiction vector: sugar. Prognosis: obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease. Life expectancy: reduced.*

Everyone was mouths. Open mouths, chewing, swallowing, shitting, eating again. The ouroboros of digestion, the endless biological imperative to consume. Ares exploded and they bought hot dogs. Iphy drowned and the world kept eating.

Children played tag, shrieking with joy. She saw their futures—climate wars, resource scarcity, the wet bulb temperature making equators uninhabitable, migration camps, starvation, the children who would never grow old because collapse doesn't wait for graduations or weddings or grandchildren.

*Anthropocene*, she thought. *Named after us. The era of human dominance. The sixth mass extinction. We eat the world and then we'll starve.*

The crowd was Lestrygonian—Joyce's cannibalistic giants, devouring everything, insatiable. The market was a feeding ground. Civilization was a thin veneer over carnage, and she'd seen through it, couldn't unsee it, the implant had torn away the veil and underneath was just hunger, just mouths, just death wearing the mask of life.

She started laughing again—then sobbing—then laughing—

"Persephone, *please*—" James's voice cracking.

She grabbed his face, made him look at the market. "Don't you see it?" Wild-eyed, desperate. "Don't you see what we are?"

He looked. Saw families, vendors, children, normalcy. "I see people living."

"No." She released him, slumped back. "We're dying. All of us. We've always been dying. This is just the intermission before the grave."

James closed his eyes. Opened them. "Then let's go home."

She had no fight left. Just exhaustion. The manic energy drained, leaving only hollow resignation. She nodded.


SCENE 6: JAMES CARES FOR HER

He drove in silence. She stared out the window at passing trees—birch, maple, ash, hemlock, all dying, all temporary, all waiting for the beetles and the fungus and the chainsaws to finish what climate change started.

The farmhouse appeared like a memory. He helped her inside, her legs unsteady, body weak from hyperventilating. Laid her on the couch. Covered her with a blanket. She didn't react. Eyes open, staring at ceiling beams, counting knots in wood. Tree corpses. We build houses from tree corpses and call it home.

James made tea. Chamomile, honey, the small rituals of care. Brought it to her. She didn't drink.

He sat on the floor beside the couch, held her hand. Didn't speak. What could he say? What words could bridge the gap between his normalcy and her abyss?

"I saw them all dying," she whispered finally.

"Who?"

"Everyone. The fish. The pig. The children. You."

"I'm not dying."

She looked at him for the first time in hours, really looked. "You are. Just slower."

James was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Then we're all dying together. You're not alone."

The absurd comfort of it. Shared mortality. Universal doom. She almost laughed again but was too tired.

They sat in silence. Time passed—sun moving across floorboards, shadows lengthening, the afternoon aging into evening. Dust motes spiraled through slant light. The earth turned. Entropy increased. The heat death of the universe approached at the speed of causality.

James fell asleep sitting against the couch, his head tilted back, breathing steady. She watched his chest rise and fall. *Respiration. Oxidation. Mitochondrial decay. He's burning himself to stay alive. We're all just slow fires.*

His hand was still holding hers. She registered the tenderness, the care, the love that couldn't save anything. He'd brought her to the cult—Apron Man, the drugging, the corruption of her implant. He was complicit. But he was also here, holding her hand while she unraveled. Both things true. Contradictions unresolved.

The cult said: *Accelerate the end. Let it burn. Dionysian expenditure, ecstatic waste, embrace the collapse.*

James said: *You're not alone.*

Which was worse? Nihilism with clarity, or compassion that changed nothing?

She closed her eyes.


SCENE 7: MIKE'S SHADOW

Time became fluid again. Was it hours later? Days? She couldn't tell. The light through the window had changed—dusk now, orange and dying, the same orange as the Ares explosion, the horizon burning with the day's last fire.

James was gone. Upstairs? Out getting groceries? It didn't matter.

Her phone buzzed on the coffee table. It had been buzzing for days, weeks, ignored, irrelevant. She didn't reach for it.

But the implant noticed. An overlay flickered across her vision: *INCOMING MESSAGE. SENDER IDENTIFIED: Mike H. Priority: High.*

Her eyes widened. First real emotion since the market—not relief, not fear. Dread.

The phone buzzed again. She sat up—first voluntary movement in hours. Reached for it with trembling hands. Read the preview:

*"I'm coming to Maine. We need to talk."*

She unlocked the phone. Read the full message:

*"Persephone, I know you're with James. I know what happened with Ares. I'm coming to get you. We can fix this. —Mike"*

Her hand trembled. The phone almost slipped from her fingers.

*He survived.* The thought came distantly, confused. *The rocket exploded. How is he alive?*

The implant provided no answers. Just cold data: *Sender location: En route. Estimated arrival: 18-24 hours. Psychological markers: Distress detected. Recommendation: Prepare for confrontation.*

She stood. Legs weak but functional. Walked to the window. Outside, the forest darkened, shadows pooling between trees. Somewhere out there, Mike was driving north. Coming for her. Agamemnon returning from failed wars, expecting welcome, finding betrayal.

He thought he could fix this. Fix *what*? Iphy was dead. The rocket exploded. The golden warblers were extinct. The planet was dying. She was dying. James was dying. Everyone in the market was dying. You couldn't fix entropy. You couldn't negotiate with thermodynamics.

But he was coming anyway.

She looked out at the darkening woods. The trees stood silent, waiting. Everything was waiting. The cult, the collapse, the inevitable confrontation.

"Agamemnon returns," she whispered to the glass.

The window reflected her face back—hollow-eyed, haunted, barely recognizable. Not Persephone anymore. Not the ornithologist from Miami, not the mother of Iphy, not the woman who once believed birds could be saved.

Someone else. Something else.

The dusk light turned blood-orange, and in the distance, a crow laughed.
